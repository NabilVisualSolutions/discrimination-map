# Discrimination Map — Pitch Script

Three versions for three situations: a 30-second intro, a 3-minute pitch for
an organization meeting, and anticipated hard questions with honest answers.
Adjust names/numbers in brackets before using.

---

## 1. The 30-second version (elevator / cold intro)

> Discrimination Map is an open map that documents hate incidents —
> racism, antisemitism, Islamophobia, homophobia, Neo-Nazi activity,
> harassment, and sexual violence — as they're reported, with a timeline you
> can scrub back through. Nothing is presented as proven; every mark is a
> documented, sourced *lead*, and it stays on the map even years later,
> fading but never disappearing, because erasing history isn't neutral.
> We're looking for [organizations to partner with / local moderators /
> people to help translate it / nothing right now, just spreading the word].

---

## 2. The 3-minute version (meeting with an NGO or civil-society group)

**Open with the problem, not the product:**

> Most hate-incident data lives in two places: locked inside a handful of
> national NGOs who track one country and one issue, or scattered across
> thousands of individual news stories and social posts that nobody's
> connecting. If you want to see the pattern — is this getting worse in this
> region, is it a wave or a one-off, does it cluster around a specific
> event — that's genuinely hard to see today.

**What it does:**

> Discrimination Map pulls together three kinds of input: public reports
> people file themselves, automated scanning of public social posts for
> relevant keywords, and — where we can integrate with an existing
> chronicle like yours — data you're already collecting. Everything lands
> on one map, color-coded by category, with a timeline slider so you can
> watch a region's pattern develop over years, not just see a snapshot.

**The part that matters most — how we handle the sensitive parts:**

> Three design decisions we treat as non-negotiable, not features:
> 1. **Nothing is a verdict.** Every automatically-detected item is labeled
>    "unverified lead," never a finding. We're documenting reports, not
>    accusing named people.
> 2. **Locations get fuzzed for the most sensitive categories** — sexual
>    assault and harassment reports never show an exact pin, only an
>    approximate area, so the map can't be used to re-identify someone.
> 3. **A quick-exit button and real crisis resources are on every screen**,
>    because we assume some visitors are looking this up about their own
>    situation, not researching it abstractly.

**The ask (customize per audience):**

> - *To an NGO with existing data:* Would you be open to a conversation
>   about surfacing your chronicle on the map, with full attribution and
>   your review process staying in your hands?
> - *To a potential moderator/volunteer:* We need people who can review
>   flagged reports in [region/language] before they go public — a few
>   hours a month, not a job.
> - *To press/media:* Would this be useful as a sourcing tool for a story
>   you're already working on?
> - *To anyone:* Even just sharing the link with people who'd find it useful
>   helps — this only gets more useful as more regions have real coverage.

## 3. Anticipated questions — honest answers

**"How do you verify reports aren't false or malicious?"**
> We don't claim to, and we say so on the map itself. Automated and
> user-submitted reports are labeled "unverified lead" and shown as
> *reports*, not facts. For the most sensitive category — sexual assault —
> we hold reports in a moderation queue before they're public at all. We'd
> rather be transparent about the limits than overclaim certainty we don't
> have.

**"Isn't this just a target list waiting to be misused?"**
> That's the risk we designed hardest against. The map documents incidents
> and public reporting — it does not store or display names, faces, or
> addresses of private individuals, on either side. Locations for the most
> sensitive categories are deliberately imprecise. If you see a way it could
> still be misused, we want to hear it before launch, not after.

**"Why keep old incidents visible instead of just showing recent activity?"**
> Because a map that only shows the last few years makes long-standing
> patterns invisible — and erasing a documented incident because it's old
> is itself an editorial choice, one we don't think we should make silently.
> We fade older entries visually so recency is still obvious, but we don't
> delete the record.

**"What's your legal exposure if someone claims defamation?"**
> This is exactly why nothing on the map is phrased as a finding of fact
> about a named individual — it documents reported incidents and links to
> the original source. We're not a court and don't claim to be one. [Adapt
> this answer with actual legal counsel before any public launch — this
> script is not legal advice.]

**"Who's behind this / is it funded / is it neutral?"**
> [Answer honestly per your actual situation — funding source and
> affiliation are exactly the kind of thing that erodes trust fast if it
> comes out later instead of upfront.]

**"Can we get the data / an API / a data-sharing agreement?"**
> [Decide this before you're asked. A reasonable default: raw data isn't
> open by default, given the sensitive categories, but aggregate,
> anonymized statistics or a formal partnership are things you're open to
> discussing.]

## Notes for whoever delivers this

- Lead with the safeguards, not just the feature list, when talking to
  NGOs — that's what earns trust with people who've seen data misused
  before.
- Don't promise verification, moderation turnaround times, or coverage in a
  specific region unless it's already true — this script is written to be
  honest about what's live vs. planned; keep it that way as the project
  evolves.
- If asked to demo it live, use the mockup's sample data and say plainly
  that it's sample data, not real incidents — don't let a demo imply more
  real coverage than exists yet.
