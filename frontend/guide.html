Guide

A shared record of discrimination, built so no one carries it alone.

What this is, how to report something that happened to you, how to read the map, and how to help.

I started this because it happened to me too. One report is easy to wave away. Thousands, on one map, are not.

What this is

The Discrimination Map collects reports of racist, antisemitic, far-right and other hate incidents and puts them on one map. Some come from people who were there. Some are pulled from public news and social posts and marked as unverified leads until a person checks them.

It is documentation, not accusation. Every entry is treated as alleged until reviewed. No names of private individuals. If you are in immediate danger, call your local emergency number first — in the EU, 112.

Report something yourself

Anonymous. No account. Takes a minute.

1

Open the report form

On the map, tap the + Report an incident button. Or drop the pin exactly where it happened: right-click anywhere on the map on a computer, or press and hold a spot on a phone.

2

Describe it and place it

Pick one or more categories, write what happened in plain words, and set the location — search for a place, use your current position, or keep the pin you dropped.

3

Set when it happened

The form has a date and time field. It does not have to be today — you can report something from weeks, months or years ago. The map's timeline uses this date.

4

Send it — and fix it later if you need to

After you submit, this device can edit the report for one hour (an "Edit my report" button appears). After that it locks, and because reports are anonymous there is no account we can use to reopen it for you.

What happens to your report

Confirmed volunteers review incoming reports and mark each one verified, declined, or not relevant. Only verified incidents show on the public map.

For the public, the exact spot is blurred by about 500 m so a single report can't pin down an address. Sensitive categories — sexual violence, harassment — are blurred much wider (~5 km) and always held for review first.

Every change to a report is logged: what changed, when, and by whom.

Reading the map

Coloured dots are incidents, one colour per category. Open a dot for the full file: what happened, why it was flagged, the possibly-applicable law, and the source.

Thin lines join incidents of the same category into one connected network, so clusters and patterns stand out at a glance. A dot that blinks between colours belongs to several categories at once.

Gold stars are solidarity events — counter-protests and vigils against the far right. A positive public record, not a harm report.

The timeline under the map filters everything by when incidents happened — drag it to look at a period. The ⚑ filter chooses which categories to show.

The Table tab is the whole dataset as a sortable spreadsheet you can export as CSV. The Law tab lists the statutes incidents are breaking, with a count for each — tap one to filter the map to those incidents.

Your safety and privacy

Reporting is anonymous. We don't ask for your name, we don't require an account, and the public never sees an exact location.

If reporting from a shared or monitored device, open a private window and close it afterwards. In an emergency, contact the police or a local counselling service first — this map is a record, not a response service.

Help keep it going

The map runs on volunteers. You choose how you want to help when you apply: reviewing incoming incidents, translation, outreach and partner organisations, research and documentation, development, or legal support.

Apply with a nabilvs.com account (any sign-in works, including Google). An admin confirms your role, and each confirmed role gives you a badge.

Ready to help?

Approved incident reviewers get a review queue right on their Pantheon dashboard — no separate login. Everything else is coordinated from there too.
